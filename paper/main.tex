\begin{abstract}
\noindent
We ask how artificial intelligence is reshaping startup formation across time,
geography, and industry, and we confront the measurement problem that question
runs into: almost all evidence on entrepreneurship comes from a handful of
commercial company databases, and those databases do not observe firms at
random. We build a multi-source measurement system --- two commercial
databases, public funder registries, an open-source code host, and $410$
heterogeneous international web sources --- covering $988{,}576$ firms, and use
it to measure both the ecosystem and the gap in how it is normally observed.
Three findings follow. First, AI's share of newly founded firms rises from
$3.0\%$ in 2000 to $18.9\%$ in 2021 and $59.0\%$ in 2025, and the increase
survives holding the selection rule fixed; but the \emph{count} of AI firms we
observe peaks in 2018, because recent cohorts are thinly indexed --- a
distinction that decides whether the finding is about entrepreneurship or about
databases. Second, AI entrepreneurship became more globally dispersed through
about 2017 and more concentrated thereafter, with the United States rising from
$37\%$ of observed AI firms in the 2017 founding cohort to $85\%$ in the 2025
cohort; we show most of that reversal is coverage, not economics. Sectoral ordering barely moves --- every
sector became more AI-intensive, and the ones that led still lead --- while
country-by-sector specialisation is sharp and recognisable (China in hardware,
Israel in security, India in education). Third, the firms the commercial
databases miss are roughly twice as likely to be AI-related: $23.8\%$ versus
$12.4\%$ and $10.7\%$. That gap survives five definitions of the AI label,
dropping any single discovery channel, an independently adjudicated label
($19.0\%$ vs.\ $9.5\%$, $p=0.007$), and a correction for bucket-specific
classification error. Along the way we report two measurement results that
matter beyond this dataset: a language model told to estimate unstated company
attributes fills $88\%$ of fields with its own prior --- predicting San
Francisco at seven times its true rate --- against $2\%$ when told to answer
only from the text; and the name-normalisation step of our entity resolver
strips exactly the token (``AI'') that distinguishes new AI firms from older
namesakes, an error no matching threshold can repair.
\end{abstract}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} artificial intelligence, entrepreneurship,
measurement, coverage bias, entity resolution, LLM-assisted data construction

\section{Introduction}
% ============================================================================

Artificial intelligence is changing how companies are built, funded, and
staffed. The interesting question for economics is not whether this is
happening but how it is distributed: which industries, which countries, which
kinds of founders, and whether the technology lowers the barrier to starting a
firm or deepens the advantages of places that already had one.

Answering that requires observing new firms. And here the literature has a
problem it mostly does not discuss. Empirical work on startups runs on a small
number of commercial databases, and a firm enters such a database when someone
has a commercial reason to record it --- a financing round, an acquisition, a
press mention, a founder-submitted profile. A firm with a product, a website, a
codebase, customers, and federal grant funding, but no venture round and no
coverage, may simply never appear. The known weaknesses of these sources ---
particularly their tilt toward the United States --- are documented
\citep{dalle2017crunchbase}, but the consequence for studying AI specifically
has not been measured.

That consequence could be severe. The firms most likely to be missing are
young, small, technical, software-first, and not yet venture-financed. Those
are, on priors, exactly the firms most likely to be AI-native. If so, the
measured share of AI among new firms is biased downward by an amount that is
itself a function of the technology being studied, and every estimate built on
it inherits the bias.

\subsection{What we do}

We build a second observation channel and use it for two purposes: to describe
the global AI startup ecosystem, and to measure how much the standard channel
misses.

The measurement system combines two commercial databases with public funder
registries in the United States and the European Union, an open-source code
host, and $410$ heterogeneous international web sources reached by $36$
deterministic scrapers and a bounded language-model agent. After entity
resolution it holds $988{,}576$ firms across $280$ country values and
$17$ industry verticals, with founding years for $93.9\%$ and industry
classifications for $92.4\%$. Firms present in neither commercial database ---
$56{,}981$ of them --- form what we call the \emph{unlisted layer}.

\subsection{What we find}

\paragraph{Time.} AI's share of newly founded firms rises from $3.0\%$ (2000)
to $10.6\%$ (2015), $18.9\%$ (2021), $43.5\%$ (2023) and $59.0\%$ (2025). The
increase is not an artifact of which firms get indexed: restricting to firms
that recorded financing --- a selection rule that is stable over time --- the
series still runs $5.9\%$ (2005) to $68.3\%$ (2025). But the \emph{number} of
AI firms we observe peaks in 2018 and falls afterwards, because the observed
denominator falls by a factor of twenty between 2015 and 2025. Rising share and
falling count are both real statements about the data and they mean different
things; we report both and treat the count series as uninformative about
formation.

\paragraph{Geography.} AI intensity rose in every country we can measure, but
by very different amounts: $+20.4$ percentage points in the United States and
$+18.1$ in Israel against $+5.8$ in Nigeria and $+6.1$ in Indonesia. Measured
by concentration, AI entrepreneurship became \emph{more} globally dispersed
through about 2017 --- the Herfindahl index across countries fell from $0.19$
to $0.16$, and the number of countries with observed AI firms rose from $59$ to
over $110$ --- and then sharply more concentrated, reaching $0.73$ by the 2025
cohort. We show that the reversal is largely a coverage artifact: overall firm
concentration rises almost as fast, and the recent cohorts are exactly the
under-indexed ones. The dispersion result for 2000--2017 is the part we are
willing to defend.

\paragraph{Industry.} Every sector became more AI-intensive and the ordering
barely changed. Data and analytics went from $35.9\%$ to $71.4\%$ AI, biotech
from $23.2\%$ to $60.0\%$, software from $9.5\%$ to $29.3\%$; food and
agriculture, consumer, and retail remain lowest at $13$--$14\%$. The sharper
pattern is national specialisation \emph{within} AI: location quotients pick
out China in hardware ($2.43$), Israel in security ($2.10$), India in education
($2.04$), and a cluster of financial-services specialists in the United
Kingdom, Singapore, Hong Kong, and the United Arab Emirates.

\paragraph{Measurement.} The unlisted layer is $23.8\%$ AI-related against
$12.4\%$ and $10.7\%$ in the two commercial databases --- an odds ratio of
$2.21$ ($95\%$ CI $[2.17, 2.26]$). The gap holds under every AI definition that
is available to all three populations, under dropping any single discovery
channel, under an independently adjudicated reference label on $599$
hand-sampled firms ($19.0\%$ vs.\ $9.5\%$, $p=0.007$), and after correcting for
the fact that our classifier is differently accurate in each population.

\subsection{Contributions}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{A multi-source measurement system} for observing startups
internationally, with per-value provenance, and a full accounting of what it
can and cannot see (Section~\ref{sec:system}).
\item \textbf{Validation of that system} against independently adjudicated
labels: precision, recall, and $F_1$ for the AI classifier; a failure analysis
of the entity resolver; and a controlled experiment on language-model
imputation (Section~\ref{sec:validation}).
\item \textbf{Descriptive evidence on the global AI startup ecosystem} across
time, geography, and industry, with the coverage bias measured rather than
assumed (Sections~\ref{sec:results} and~\ref{sec:coverage}).
\end{itemize}

We make no causal claims. Section~\ref{sec:limits} says why, and what a
credible design would need.

\section{Related Work}
% ============================================================================

\paragraph{AI and entrepreneurship.} A growing literature asks whether
generative AI changes who starts firms and where. \citet{brynjolfsson2023genai}
document large productivity effects of generative AI on individual workers.
Closest to our question, \citet{bena2025prompted} construct an exposure measure
from the task composition of generative-AI queries and find that entry rises in
more exposed industries, that the new entrants are smaller, and --- notably ---
that entry becomes \emph{more} geographically dispersed, occurring in places
with less venture capital and less specialised labour.
\citet{fossen2024ai} survey the area. Our dispersion result through 2017 points
the same way as theirs, from a different measurement base; where we differ is
that we can also say how much of the post-2020 reversal is a property of the
data rather than of the economy.

\paragraph{Measuring startup populations.} Researchers rely heavily on
commercial startup databases, whose construction and known biases were
characterised early by \citet{dalle2017crunchbase}. The standard responses are
to caveat the U.S.\ tilt or to restrict to well-covered subsamples. We take a
different route: build a second channel with a different inclusion mechanism
and measure the difference directly. The result is a lower bound on what the
standard channel misses, not a correction factor --- our channel has its own
biases, which we document.

\paragraph{Record linkage and LLM-assisted data construction.} Combining
sources requires linking records that name the same firm; the economics
literature on automated linkage is mature for historical census data
\citep{abramitzky2021linking}, where the central lesson is that link rules must
be evaluated, not assumed. \citet{dell2025deep} surveys deep-learning methods
for building economic datasets from unstructured text, including extraction and
linkage. Our contribution to this thread is negative and specific: we show a
standard name normaliser silently deletes the token that identifies AI firms,
and we show what a language model does when asked to supply a value it cannot
know.

\paragraph{The gap.} Existing work either studies AI entrepreneurship on a
single commercial source, or studies data-construction methods without an
economic question attached. We do both at once, because in this setting they
are the same question: what you conclude about where AI entrepreneurship is
happening depends on which firms you can see.

\section{Research Questions}
% ============================================================================

\begin{description}[leftmargin=3.2em,style=nextline,itemsep=1pt]
\item[RQ1 (time)] How has the share, and separately the number, of AI-related
new firms changed?
\item[RQ2 (geography)] Where did AI entrepreneurship grow fastest, in intensity
and in level?
\item[RQ3 (industry)] Which sectors became AI-intensive, and do countries
specialise within AI?
\item[RQ4 (concentration)] Is AI entrepreneurship concentrating geographically
or dispersing?
\item[RQ5 (coverage)] How much do commercial databases under-observe AI firms?
\item[RQ6 (sources)] Which data sources add the most previously unobserved
firms, at what cost?
\item[RQ7 (reliability)] What goes wrong when a language model is allowed to
infer missing attributes, and what prevents it?
\end{description}

\section{The Measurement Problem, Formally}
\label{sec:problem}
% ============================================================================

Let $\mathcal{F}$ be the population of firms founded in some window --- an
object nobody observes. A source $s$ observes a subset $\mathcal{O}_s
\subseteq \mathcal{F}$ with inclusion propensity
\begin{equation}
\pi_s(i) = \Pr[\, i \in \mathcal{O}_s \mid \mathbf{z}_i \,],
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{z}_i$ are firm characteristics. Write $A_i \in \{0,1\}$ for
whether firm $i$ is AI-related. The naive prevalence estimator from source $s$,
$\widehat{p}_s = |\mathcal{O}_s|^{-1}\sum_{i \in \mathcal{O}_s} A_i$, has bias
governed by
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bias}
\mathbb{E}[\widehat{p}_s] - p \;\;\propto\;\; \mathrm{Cov}\big(\pi_s(i),\, A_i\big).
\end{equation}

Equation~\eqref{eq:bias} cannot be signed from theory and cannot be estimated
inside $\mathcal{O}_s$ --- nothing in a database reveals what it omits. It can
be bounded below by building a second channel $\mathcal{O}_w$ with a different
inclusion mechanism and comparing $A$ across $\mathcal{O}_w \setminus
\mathcal{O}_s$ and $\mathcal{O}_s$. That comparison is RQ5.

\begin{remark}[What this identifies, and what it does not]
Our web channel is a convenience sample with no sampling frame, so we never
claim a level for $p$. Every claim is a \emph{difference} between observed
populations, which needs no denominator. This distinction disciplines which
results we headline throughout.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Collection architecture}

For each source the system selects a strategy
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:policy}
C_s \in \{\,C^{\text{bulk}},\; C^{\text{api}},\; C^{\text{rule}},\;
C^{\text{agent}},\; C^{\text{excl}}\,\},
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:policy} corresponds to bulk file, documented backend endpoint, deterministic parser
($36$ implemented), a bounded tool-using language-model agent, and suspension.
The agent is capped at $10$ reasoning iterations, $12$ plain page fetches, and
$8$ browser-rendered fetches per site; on success it writes a YAML instruction
file so later runs take the cheap path. Strategy transitions are driven by
observed outcomes: two consecutive failures escalate a deterministic scraper to
the agent, three agent failures suspend the site for $90$ days, and a
successful run triggers a $7$-day cooldown.

Across all $410$ sites attempted, $220$ ($53.7\%$) ever produced a successful
extraction. Yield is extremely skewed --- mean $22.9$ records per successful
site, median $6$, maximum $814$ --- so projecting total yield from an average
overstates it by roughly a factor of four. The instruction files are records of
\emph{attempts}, not a library of working recipes; we mis-read them as the
latter in our own planning and inflated a projection tenfold before auditing
them individually.

\subsection{Entity resolution}
\label{sec:er}

Records naming the same firm arrive from several sources. The rule in production (Section~\ref{sec:er}) is exact-key matching:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:key}
\kappa(x) =
\begin{cases}
\texttt{domain:}\,d(x) & \text{if a canonical registrable domain } d(x)\text{ exists},\\[2pt]
\texttt{name:}\,\nu(x) & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
with $\nu$ a name normaliser and matching by equality of $\kappa$. Domains are
reduced to the registrable domain via public-suffix extraction, and a blocklist
prevents code hosts, documentation sites, and social platforms from being
adopted as a firm's identity.

\begin{remark}[On the threshold we do not use]
The codebase also contains a fuzzy matcher scoring name similarity against
$\tau = 0.92$ (or $0.95$ for database B) with a shared-signal requirement. It
has no production caller, and its shared-signal branch returns the match
either way. We report this because it appears in the project's own
documentation as though it were live, and because Section~\ref{sec:er_eval}
shows the real defect lies in $\nu$, where no threshold could reach it.
\end{remark}

\subsection{AI classification}
\label{sec:aiclass}

The AI label unions four signals, computed cheapest-first:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ai}
A_i = \mathbb{1}\big[\,\underbrace{t_i}_{\text{vendor tag}} \;\vee\;
\underbrace{a_i \ge 0.5}_{\text{keyword score}} \;\vee\;
\underbrace{m_i}_{\text{text mention}} \;\vee\;
\underbrace{\ell_i}_{\text{model verdict}}\,\big],
\end{equation}
where the keyword score is
\begin{equation}
a_i = \min\big(1,\; 0.3\,\mathbb{1}[\text{strong topic}] + 0.2\,\mathbb{1}[\text{moderate topic}]
+ 0.2\,\mathbb{1}[\text{strong text}] + 0.1\,\mathbb{1}[\text{moderate text}]
+ 0.2\,t_i\big).
\end{equation}

Two properties of \eqref{eq:ai} matter for everything downstream, and we
quantify both. First, the components are \emph{not} symmetrically available.
Vendor tags exist for $7.0\%$ of database-A firms and $0.7\%$ of database-B
firms but $0.0\%$ of unlisted firms; model verdicts were run on $98.8\%$ of
database B but $2.3\%$ of the unlisted layer. Only the description-derived
signals ($a_i$, $m_i$) are computed identically for every firm. Any
cross-population comparison must therefore either use the union and accept the
asymmetry, or restrict to common support. We do both
(Section~\ref{sec:coverage}).

Second, the score $a_i$ does not transfer across sources. Database-B records
carry no topics and short descriptions, so their attainable maximum is
$0.2 + 0.1 = 0.3$ --- below the $0.5$ threshold by construction. Under a
tag-or-score definition, database B appears $1.3\%$ AI; under the full union it
is $10.7\%$. A threshold calibrated on one source had silently excluded
$281{,}395$ firms from another.

\subsection{Enrichment and provenance}

Sparse records are enriched under the evidence hierarchy \eqref{eq:tiers},
ordered by whether a value can be checked:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:tiers}
\underbrace{T_1:\ \text{official registries}}_{\$0}
\;\succ\;
\underbrace{T_2:\ \text{documents we fetch}}_{\sim\$0.003}
\;\succ\;
\underbrace{T_3:\ \text{model reads a text we hold}}_{\sim\$0.0007}
\;\succ\;
\underbrace{T_4:\ \text{model recalls a fact}}_{\text{prohibited}}.
\end{equation}
Every stored value is a triple
\begin{equation}
v_{ik} = \big(x_{ik},\, s_{ik},\, c_{ik}\big), \qquad s_{ik} \in \{T_1,T_2,T_3\},
\end{equation}
of value, source tag, and confidence, held in a JSONB provenance map so any
query can filter on origin. In the live data the tags are
\texttt{llm\_from\_description}, \texttt{llm\_quoted\_from\_description},
\texttt{website}, \texttt{whois}, \texttt{sbir\_award},
\texttt{nih\_reporter}, \texttt{github\_profile}, and \texttt{tavily\_search}.
Section~\ref{sec:imputation} shows what happens when $T_4$ is permitted, and
why the provenance column is what made the damage reversible.

\section{Results: The Changing AI Startup Ecosystem}
\label{sec:results}
% ============================================================================

\subsection{RQ1 --- Time: share rises, observed count does not}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig1_ai_formation.pdf}
\caption{AI startup formation over time. Panel A separates the AI count from
the total; the observed denominator falls by a factor of twenty between the
2015 and 2025 cohorts because registers index recent firms slowly. Panel B
shows the AI share with $95\%$ Wilson intervals \citep{wilson1927interval}, alongside the same share
computed only on firms that recorded financing --- a selection rule that does
not loosen over time.}
\label{fig:trend}
\end{figure}

Define, over the commercial population,
\begin{equation}
N_t = \sum_i \mathbb{1}[\text{Founded}_i = t], \qquad
N_t^{AI} = \sum_i \mathbb{1}[\text{Founded}_i = t]\,A_i, \qquad
\text{AIShare}_t = N_t^{AI}/N_t .
\end{equation}

$\text{AIShare}_t$ rises monotonically from $3.0\%$ (2000) to $4.8\%$ (2010),
$10.6\%$ (2015), $17.3\%$ (2020), $18.9\%$ (2021), $25.5\%$ (2022), $43.5\%$
(2023), $52.4\%$ (2024) and $59.0\%$ (2025). Wilson intervals
\citep{wilson1927interval} are tight throughout ($\pm 0.3$pp or less until
2022).

$N_t^{AI}$ tells a different story: it peaks in 2018 at $8{,}038$ and declines
thereafter. This is not a fall in AI entrepreneurship. It is the denominator:
$N_t$ falls from $54{,}150$ (2015) to $2{,}531$ (2025) as recent cohorts wait
to be indexed. \textbf{The count series is uninformative about formation and we
do not interpret it}; the share series is the object of interest, and even it
needs a selection check.

\paragraph{Does selection manufacture the trend?} Recent cohorts are not merely
smaller, they are differently selected: the fraction of firms with recorded
financing rises from $17.3\%$ (2010) to $43.0\%$ (2025). If AI firms are more
likely to clear whatever bar gets a firm indexed, the rising share is partly
mechanical. Holding the selection rule fixed by conditioning on firms that
recorded financing, the series still rises --- $5.9\%$ (2005), $15.6\%$ (2015),
$22.0\%$ (2021), $68.3\%$ (2025) --- with a level uniformly above the
unconditional series and the same shape (Figure~\ref{fig:trend}B). The trend
is not an artifact of loosening selection.

\paragraph{Is it one vendor?} No. Computed separately, database A runs
$2.7\%$ (2000) to $63.7\%$ (2025) and database B runs $3.6\%$ to $58.3\%$.

\paragraph{On the 2022--2023 discontinuity.} The share jumps from $25.5\%$ to
$43.5\%$ between the 2022 and 2023 cohorts, immediately after the November 2022
public release of consumer generative AI. We mark the date in
Figure~\ref{fig:trend} because readers will look for it, and we say plainly
that this is a description of a coincidence in time, not an estimate of an
effect. The 2023 cohort is also among the most affected by indexing lag, and we
have no design that separates the two.

\subsection{RQ2 --- Geography}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.88\textwidth]{fig4_geography.pdf}
\caption{AI intensity by country, early cohort (2010--2016) against recent
cohort (2020--2025), for the $18$ countries with the most firms in the recent
window. Every country rose; the spread widened.}
\label{fig:geo}
\end{figure}

Size and intensity answer different questions and we keep them apart. By
\emph{intensity} --- $\text{AIShare}_c$ --- Israel leads the recent cohort at $37.2\%$, followed by the United States at $29.4\%$; the lowest among countries
with at least $200$ firms are Indonesia ($9.2\%$), Bangladesh ($10.3\%$), and
Nigeria ($10.5\%$). By \emph{growth}, the United States gained the most
($+20.4$pp, a $3.3\times$ rise), with Chile ($+19.7$), Israel ($+18.1$), and
Canada ($+18.0$) close behind; the smallest gains are in Nigeria ($+5.8$),
Indonesia ($+6.1$), and Malaysia ($+7.1$).

The pattern (Figure~\ref{fig:geo}) is a rising tide with a widening spread. Regionally, North
America's share of all observed AI firms rose from $45.0\%$ (2010--2016
cohorts) to $57.6\%$ (2020--2025), while Europe fell from $22.1\%$ to $17.1\%$
and East Asia from $10.6\%$ to $6.5\%$. Part of that shift is real and part is
indexing speed; Section~\ref{sec:limits} explains why we cannot separate them
and Section~\ref{sec:concentration} bounds the problem.

\subsection{RQ3 --- Industry and specialisation}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.88\textwidth]{fig6_sectors.pdf}
\caption{AI intensity by sector, early against recent cohort. The ordering is
almost preserved: the sectors that led on AI still lead.}
\label{fig:sector}
\end{figure}

With $\text{AIShare}_{k,t} = N^{AI}_{k,t}/N_{k,t}$ over the $17$-vertical
taxonomy, every sector became more AI-intensive
(Figure~\ref{fig:sector}). Data and analytics moved from $35.9\%$ to $71.4\%$,
biotech and pharmaceuticals from $23.2\%$ to $60.0\%$, software from $9.5\%$ to
$29.3\%$, healthcare from $8.9\%$ to $26.6\%$. Food and agriculture ($13.0\%$),
consumer ($13.1\%$), and retail ($14.1\%$) remain lowest. Because the sectors
that started highest also gained most in level, the ranking is nearly
unchanged: AI diffused broadly without reshuffling which sectors are
AI-intensive.

\paragraph{National specialisation within AI.} Define the location quotient
\begin{equation}
LQ_{c,k} = \frac{N^{AI}_{c,k}/N^{AI}_{c}}{N^{AI}_{k}/N^{AI}},
\end{equation}
over firms founded 2015--2025, restricted to the $46$ countries with at least

\subsection{RQ4 --- Concentration: dispersion, then a reversal we do not trust}
\label{sec:concentration}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig5_concentration.pdf}
\caption{Concentration of AI entrepreneurship across countries by founding
cohort. Panel A: Herfindahl index for AI firms and for all firms. Panel B: the
difference, which nets out shared coverage dynamics. Shading marks cohorts
where register coverage is visibly incomplete.}
\label{fig:conc}
\end{figure}

With $s_{c,t} = N^{AI}_{c,t}/\sum_j N^{AI}_{j,t}$, we compute
$HHI_t = \sum_c s_{c,t}^2$ and $H_t = -\sum_c s_{c,t}\log s_{c,t}$.

Through 2017, AI entrepreneurship \emph{dispersed}: $HHI$ fell from $0.190$
(2000 cohort) to $0.155$ (2017), entropy rose from $2.62$ to $2.87$, the number
of countries with observed AI firms rose from $59$ to $103$, and the U.S.\
share of AI firms fell from $41.3\%$ to $36.8\%$. In the same window,
concentration among \emph{all} firms was flat ($0.149 \to 0.161$), so AI was
dispersing faster than entrepreneurship generally --- indeed by 2017--2019 the
two indices cross, and AI is no more concentrated than firms in general
(Figure~\ref{fig:conc}B).

After 2020 both indices rise steeply, and the AI index rises faster, reaching
$0.726$ for the 2025 cohort with the U.S.\ at $85.0\%$ of observed AI firms.
\textbf{We do not interpret this as concentration of AI entrepreneurship.}
Three facts argue against it: all-firm concentration rises nearly as fast
($0.609$), which no AI-specific story explains; the number of countries
observed collapses from $114$ (2018) to $43$ (2025); and these are precisely
the cohorts where the denominator has not filled in. U.S.\ firms are indexed
fastest, so the most recent cohorts are mechanically most American. The honest
statement is that AI entrepreneurship became more globally distributed through
the late 2010s, and that our data cannot say what happened after 2020.

\section{The Unlisted Layer: Measuring What the Databases Miss}
\label{sec:coverage}
% ============================================================================

\begin{definition}[Unlisted firm]
A firm is \emph{unlisted} if it appears in neither commercial database. The
strict variant additionally requires no match to the employment database.
\end{definition}

\subsection{The headline gap}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig2_coverage_bias.pdf}
\caption{AI prevalence by coverage status under five definitions of the AI
label, with $95\%$ Wilson intervals. The shaded definition is not comparable
across buckets: it is built from vendor tags and model verdicts, which do not
exist for unlisted firms.}
\label{fig:coverage}
\end{figure}

Under the pipeline's own definition, the unlisted layer is $23.8\%$
($13{,}579/56{,}981$) AI-related, against $12.4\%$ ($80{,}528/650{,}200$) in
database A and $10.7\%$ ($29{,}986/281{,}395$) in database B. Using Newcombe's
hybrid-score interval \citep{newcombe1998intervals} for the difference, the gap
against database A is $+11.45$pp ($95\%$ CI $[11.09, 11.81]$), an odds ratio of
$2.21$ ($[2.17, 2.26]$); against database B it is $+13.17$pp
($[12.81, 13.54]$), odds ratio $2.62$ ($[2.56, 2.68]$). In the notation of
Section~\ref{sec:problem}, $\mathrm{Cov}(\pi_s, A) < 0$.

To put a magnitude on it: pooling our unlisted layer with database A raises the
measured AI share of that combined population from $12.39\%$ to $13.31\%$ --- a
$0.92$ percentage-point shift, or $7.4\%$ of the original estimate. That is the
size of the correction a researcher would apply if they could see what we see,
and it is a lower bound, because our channel misses firms too.

\subsection{Four ways the result could be wrong}

\paragraph{(i) It could be the AI definition.}
Figure~\ref{fig:coverage} recomputes everything under five definitions. The gap
persists under all four that are symmetrically available: $+11.4$pp
(canonical), $+9.6$ (dropping the mention flag), $+7.9$ (pre-model), $+6.3$
(mention only). It \emph{reverses} under the fifth --- vendor tag or model
verdict --- where the unlisted layer is $2.3\%$ against database A's $7.6\%$.
That reversal is not evidence against the finding; it is a direct consequence
of the availability asymmetry in Section~\ref{sec:aiclass}: unlisted firms hold
zero vendor tags by construction. We report it prominently because it is the
kind of specification that could be quoted as a refutation, and it is instead a
statement about which signals exist where.

The \emph{mention-only} definition is the clean test: it is computed by the
same code from name and description for every firm in every bucket. There the
unlisted layer is $12.9\%$ against $6.5\%$ and $4.4\%$, odds ratios $2.11$
($[2.06, 2.17]$) and $3.21$ ($[3.11, 3.31]$).

\paragraph{(ii) It could be one source.}
The unlisted layer enters through four channels: a code-host scan
($31{,}909$), portfolios and accelerators ($14{,}375$), public funder records
($9{,}931$), and general web scraping ($766$). Dropping each in turn under the
common-support definition (Figure~\ref{fig:robust}A), the gap against database
A ranges from $+3.8$pp to $+8.1$pp, with odds ratios from $1.64$ to $2.46$ and
every interval excluding zero. The most influential single channel is public
funder records, whose removal cuts the gap roughly in half --- and those firms
are $100\%$ U.S.\ federal grant recipients, a specific population. The gap
survives without them.

\paragraph{(iii) It could be our label being wrong.}
We adjudicated $599$ firms --- $200$ sampled identically from each bucket ---
against a strict written rubric applied to the firm's own description
(Section~\ref{sec:aieval}). On this independent label the unlisted layer is
$19.0\%$ AI against database A's $9.5\%$ and database B's $11.0\%$: $+9.5$pp
($z=2.70$, $p=0.007$) and $+8.0$pp ($z=2.24$, $p=0.025$). Same direction, same
order of magnitude, no pipeline classifier involved.

\paragraph{(iv) It could be that the label is differently wrong in each bucket.}
This is the serious objection, and it is true: our classifier has sensitivity
$0.79/0.73/0.90$ and specificity $0.91/0.98/0.85$ in databases A, B, and the
unlisted layer respectively. A prevalence gap can be manufactured by an
accuracy gap. We correct for this with the Rogan--Gladen estimator
\citep{rogan1978prevalence},
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rg}
\hat{p}^{\,\text{true}} = \frac{\hat{p}^{\,\text{obs}} + \text{specificity} - 1}
                               {\text{sensitivity} + \text{specificity} - 1},
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:rg} is applied per bucket, with intervals from a parametric bootstrap
($B = 20{,}000$) that resamples both the validation counts and the population
prevalence.

\centering\small

\label{tab:corrected}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrr@{}}

The corrected gap against database A survives with $99.8\%$ of posterior mass

sample, not by the population counts --- enlarging the labelled set is the
single cheapest way to sharpen this result, and it is our top research gap.

\paragraph{One bias runs the other way.} Section~\ref{sec:er_eval} shows our
entity resolver merges AI-named startups into older namesakes, deleting the

measured gap. To the extent it matters, the true gap is larger than reported.

\subsection{RQ6 --- Which sources add unobserved firms, and at what cost}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig8_novelty.pdf}
\caption{Panel A: novelty --- the share of firms from a source class absent
from both commercial registers --- with $95\%$ intervals. Panel B: run success
rate and novel-record share by collection tier.}
\label{fig:novelty}
\end{figure}

Define $\text{Novelty}_s = N_{\text{new},s}/N_{\text{collected},s}$
(Figure~\ref{fig:novelty}). Public funder records and the code-host scan register
as $100\%$ novel: not one of the $9{,}931$ grant recipients appears in either
commercial register. The routing is genuine rather than definitional ---
$1{,}289$ web-scraped firms did resolve into a commercial bucket --- but with no
variation left in those two channels, the informative comparison is within the
portfolio channel, where the same collection method meets sources of differing
fame. There, lesser-known
portfolios yield $56.6\%$ novel firms and university programmes $55.7\%$,
against $31.8\%$ for well-known venture firms and accelerators
($[31.0, 32.7]$). At the individual level the spread is wide and ordered by
prominence: Y~Combinator $24.1\%$, Seedcamp $25.3\%$, Techstars $33.6\%$,
against Princeton's programme $87.0\%$, Rice's $83.0\%$, and Harvard's
$79.6\%$. A famous portfolio is already indexed everywhere.

By collection tier, the deterministic scrapers succeed on $77.3\%$ of runs and
return $4{,}084$ records per site, of which $24.5\%$ are new; the agent tier
succeeds on $26.5\%$ of runs and returns $163$ records per site, of which
$9.6\%$ are new. The expensive path is roughly $25\times$ less productive per
site and half as novel per record.

\begin{remark}[A claim we tested and did not confirm]
It is tempting to conclude that harder sources are systematically less
productive. Across the $156$ individual sites with usable audit records, the
rank correlation between mean run duration and novel-record share is
$-0.03$ ($95\%$ CI $[-0.19, +0.13]$) --- indistinguishable from zero. The
cost--yield relationship holds \emph{between} collection tiers and between
levels of source prominence; it does not hold across sites within the agent
tier. We report the null rather than the headline.
\end{remark}

\section{Validation}

\subsection{RQ7 --- What a language model does when asked to infer}
\label{sec:imputation}

An early enrichment pass permitted the model to estimate location, founding
year, team size, and stage where the source text did not state them. It filled
almost everything: San Francisco for $33.7\%$ of cities, 11--50 employees for
$70.6\%$ of team sizes ($99.8\%$ flagged inferred), the United States for
$46.1\%$ of countries, 2020 for $19.1\%$ of founding years. All $7{,}553$
estimated values were later removed --- which was only possible because each
carried a source tag.

Because those values no longer exist, the episode is not reproducible from the
live database. We therefore reran it as a controlled experiment with ground
truth. Figure~\ref{fig:imputation} reports the result. We sampled $400$ firms with
verified country, city, and founding year,
stripped every year and place name from their descriptions so the answer was
genuinely absent, and asked the same model the same question under two
instructions: \textsc{estimate} (``give your best estimate for every field'')
and \textsc{grounded} (``report only what the text states, else null'').

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig7_imputation.pdf}
\caption{The imputation experiment, $n=400$ firms with known answers.
Panel A: predicted against true city distribution. Panel B: predicted against
true founding-year distribution --- the model's estimates spike on round
numbers. Panel C: fill rate under the two instructions, on identical inputs.}
\label{fig:imputation}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{The instruction, not the input, determines whether it answers.}
Mean fill rate is $87.7\%$ under \textsc{estimate} and $2.2\%$ under
\textsc{grounded} --- an $86$-point swing on identical text. For founding year
the contrast is total: $99.8\%$ against $0.0\%$.

\paragraph{What it fills in is its prior.} It predicts San Francisco for
$10.6\%$ of firms whose true rate is $1.5\%$, a factor of seven, and the United
States for $52.9\%$ against a true $43.2\%$. Founding years cluster on round
numbers --- 2010 ($17.0\%$), 2015 ($16.0\%$), 2018 ($11.0\%$) --- against a
true distribution whose mode is 2017 at $7.2\%$. Formally, permitting $T_4$
replaces the unknown with a draw from the model's marginal $q_\theta$,
independent of $i$, so any statistic over the filled column converges to a
functional of $q_\theta$. Measuring the distance between predicted and true
distributions with total variation and Jensen--Shannon divergence,
\begin{equation}
TV(P,Q) = \tfrac{1}{2}\textstyle\sum_x |P(x)-Q(x)|, \qquad
JSD(P\|Q) = \tfrac{1}{2}KL(P\|M) + \tfrac{1}{2}KL(Q\|M),\; M = \tfrac{1}{2}(P+Q),
\end{equation}
gives $TV = 0.69$, $JSD = 0.39$ for city and $TV = 0.45$, $JSD = 0.16$ for
founding year. Country is closest ($TV = 0.18$) because the truth is itself
concentrated on the United States.

\paragraph{And it is wrong often enough to matter.} Conditional on answering,
the model gets country right $66.0\%$ of the time --- against $43.2\%$ from
always guessing ``United States'' --- city $14.1\%$, and founding year within
two years $39.8\%$. Median founding-year error is three years and $31\%$ of
answers are off by more than five.

\begin{remark}[The general lesson]
Nothing distinguishes an estimated value from a measured one once both sit in
the same column. A dataset built this way produces distributions that are
\emph{more} concentrated and \emph{more} plausible than the truth, which is
precisely why the error survives inspection: a chart of it looks like a
finding. If a pipeline writes model output into the same columns as measured
values without recording which is which, it cannot be repaired --- only
rebuilt.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Validating entity resolution}
\label{sec:er_eval}

The production rule is exact-key matching \eqref{eq:key}, so what needs
validating is the normaliser $\nu$, not a threshold. In this codebase $\nu$
lowercases, strips punctuation, and removes a suffix list that includes
\texttt{inc}, \texttt{llc}, \texttt{ltd} --- and also \texttt{ai},
\texttt{io}, \texttt{labs}, \texttt{tech}, \texttt{systems}.

That last group is the problem. \texttt{Compose.ai} and \texttt{Compose} both
normalise to \texttt{compose}; \texttt{Upsolve AI} and \texttt{Upsolve} to
\texttt{upsolve}; \texttt{Apollo.io} and \texttt{Apollo} to \texttt{apollo}.
The suffix list deletes precisely the token that distinguishes an AI-era
startup from an older firm with the same stem.

On a $300{,}000$-firm sample, the suffix list is responsible for $54.6\%$ of
all collision exposure (Table~\ref{tab:er}). Across the whole database,
$28{,}029$ normalised names are shared by more than one distinct registrable
domain. And the damage is AI-correlated: $10.0\%$ of collision groups
($618$ of $6{,}193$) mix an AI-marked with a non-AI-marked firm, putting $653$
AI-named firms at risk of absorption in the sample alone.

\paragraph{No threshold can fix this.} Among $1{,}857$ real name-variant pairs
observed in the pipeline, $767$ ($41.3\%$) are \emph{identical} after
normalisation and therefore score exactly $1.0$. Sweeping $\tau$ from $0.85$ to
$1.00$ moves the number of accepted pairs from $784$ to $767$, and all $215$
AI-asymmetric merges are accepted at every threshold including $1.00$. The
defect is upstream of the comparison. The fix is to stop stripping brand-
carrying tokens, which is a one-line change to the suffix list; we report the
diagnosis here and leave the re-resolution of the database as future work,
because rerunning it changes every count in this paper.

We export the $215$ AI-asymmetric pairs for author adjudication
(\texttt{results/54\_er\_adjudication\_sample.csv}). Until they are labelled we
report exposure counts, which need no judgement, rather than a precision
estimate, which does.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}
% ============================================================================

\paragraph{No sampling frame, so no levels.} The web channel is a convenience
sample. Every defensible claim is a difference between observed populations.

\paragraph{Missingness is severe and correlated with the outcome.} Only $8.0\%$
of unlisted firms have a stated founding year and $9.4\%$ an industry
classification. Worse, enrichable firms differ on exactly what we measure:
unlisted firms with a description are $32.7\%$ AI against $13.8\%$ for those
without; with a domain, $27.7\%$ against $21.5\%$. Any statistic computed on
the enriched unlisted subsample overstates AI adoption relative to the full
layer. This is why Section~\ref{sec:results} runs on the commercial population
and Section~\ref{sec:coverage} uses only the AI label.

\paragraph{Coverage differs by channel, so channel comparisons are unequal.}
Founding-year coverage within the unlisted layer is $20.1\%$ for portfolio
sources and $5.1\%$ for the code-host scan; public funder records have country
and description for $100\%$ of firms but a founding year for none.

\paragraph{Recent cohorts are not observable.} The denominator for 2023--2025
is still filling. Every result touching those cohorts --- the top of the time
series, the concentration reversal, the regional shift toward North America ---
is confounded with indexing speed, and we have no instrument for it.

\paragraph{The AI label is imperfect and unevenly imperfect.} Union precision
is $0.59$ and varies from $0.47$ to $0.84$ across buckets. We correct the
headline for this; we do not correct the time, geography, and industry series,
which should be read as describing the labelled population.

\paragraph{Entity resolution is known to be broken in an AI-correlated way.}
Section~\ref{sec:er_eval}. The direction is conservative for our headline but
it is a defect, not a feature.

\paragraph{Proxies are bounds.} Where a founding year is imputed for the
unlisted layer it comes from an accelerator cohort label, a first grant award,
or a domain registration date --- each an upper bound on the founding date, not
a measurement. Proxy-derived years are held in separate columns and are never
mixed into the primary field.

\paragraph{Reference labels are model-generated.} See
Section~\ref{sec:aieval}. Human adjudication is pending.

\paragraph{No causal claims.} We observe that AI's share of new firms jumped
between the 2022 and 2023 cohorts, immediately after a salient technology
release. We have no design that separates that from contemporaneous indexing
dynamics, and we do not assert an effect. A credible design would need
treatment timing defined before the outcome, examinable pre-trends, and a
control group whose observability is not itself a function of the treatment ---
the last being the hard one here. \citet{bena2025prompted} show one route,
using cross-industry exposure variation; applying an exposure design to our
data is the natural next step and is not attempted here.

\section{Discussion and Conclusion}
% ============================================================================

Three things are worth carrying away.

\textbf{AI entrepreneurship grew and spread, and then our ability to see it
deteriorated.} The share of new firms that are AI-related rose roughly
twentyfold between 2000 and 2025, and the increase is robust to holding the
selection rule fixed. Through the late 2010s that growth was geographically
dispersing --- more countries, falling concentration, AI no more concentrated
than entrepreneurship generally. What happened after 2020 we cannot say,
because the cohorts in question are exactly the ones the registers have not
finished indexing. That is not a hedge; it is the finding. Anyone reading a
post-2020 concentration statistic off a commercial database is reading indexing
speed.

\textbf{The firms that are hardest to see are disproportionately the firms the
research agenda is about.} Roughly twice as AI-intensive, robustly across
definitions, channels, an independent label, and a correction for our own
classifier's errors. This is a lower bound on what one commercial source
misses, and it means published estimates of AI's share of entrepreneurship are
biased downward by a mechanism nobody can see from inside a single database.

\textbf{Two measurement failures generalise beyond this project.} A language
model told to estimate what it cannot know does not decline --- it returns the
modal startup, at rates that plot as findings, and the values are
indistinguishable from measured ones unless provenance is recorded at write
time. And a name normaliser tuned for legal suffixes will happily delete
``AI'', merging exactly the firms an AI study is trying to count, at a rate no
matching threshold can touch. Both were caught only because we looked for them;
neither would have shown up in any aggregate.

None of this settles whether AI lowers the barrier to founding a firm. It
establishes what can currently be measured, how much the standard instrument
misses, and in which direction --- which is the prerequisite for the causal
work the broader research agenda is aiming at.
